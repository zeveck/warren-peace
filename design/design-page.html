Warren Peace

A war between warrens, a door that was never meant to open, and the long way down to peace.

Game design Isometric pixel-art RPG with a light army war Desktop browser About 4 to 4.5 hours Reviewed in 2 rounds

The brief

Warren Peace is a large, cozy-epic isometric pixel-art adventure RPG with a light army-building war. A young rabbit whose warren is wrecked by maddened neighbours goes down through a door that was never meant to open. There he gathers friends from storybook worlds and from the strange deep places beneath every warren, and builds an army that frees each warren in turn, until all of them stand together on the downs at peace.

The feeling. Warm grass, low sun, friends who tease each other, and a growing band of oddballs who'd follow you anywhere. Underneath runs a quiet chill that gets colder with every layer down: carvings that look back, whispers in tunnels, wrong-angled rooms. Eerie, never gory, never hopeless. The war is sad rather than cruel: the enemy rabbits are victims to be woken, not villains to be killed. All ages.

Who and where. Players of all ages who like cozy RPGs and storybooks; a parent and child could play it together. Desktop browser with keyboard and mouse. Sessions of 20 to 45 minutes, with saves anywhere on the surface and autosaves at every door.

Decisions made for the requester

  • The creature. The Undersleeper is a vast, ancient dreamer beneath all the depths. It isn't evil: digging too deep disturbs its sleep, and its bad dreams leak upward as the Hum, a drone that makes rabbits forget their names and want only to dig.
  • How it's broken. Not by killing: Hum-taken rabbits are woken in battle. In the finale every freed warren drums the old warning-thump as one, while Bramble tells the sleeper the story of the journey until it dreams a kind dream.
  • No killing anywhere. Enemies are woken, fade back into the dark, or are sent packing. Fallen allies just rest.
  • Hero: Bramble, a young buck with one floppy ear. All other names are invented or come from the public-domain books, which are used as originally written (in Oz the shoes are silver).
  • Visible enemies, no random battles, so there's less grinding and the game is easier to test.
  • Quality first. The requester said quality and completeness matter more than cost.

Effort at a glance

The game is big in every direction: about 45 maps, more than 100 named characters, 47 kinds of foe, 6 army battles and roughly 440 pieces of art. The heaviest work is the writing and the art pipeline; the riskiest parts are keeping hundreds of generated images looking like one game and making the army battles fair.

The riskiest parts, and the plan for each

  1. One look across about 440 generated images. Every figure is made from one style anchor (Bramble), reduced through one pixel pipeline to one pixel size and one 48-colour palette. A check fails any sprite whose eyes vanish at small sizes, and each world's first sheet is approved before the rest are made.
  2. Fair army battles. The three-way matchup broke in review: flyers lost to the sturdy squads they're meant to beat. It was fixed and proved with a duel simulation. A balance tool now checks that every required battle is winnable with only the required recruits.
  3. Isometric drawing order. Long props like the tea table drew over Bramble. The rule is now that every multi-tile prop is cut into one-tile strips, and walkable pieces (the raft, the tree-bridge) are part of the ground.
  4. Softlocks in the puzzle worlds. Size changes come from refilling sources and reset on every map change. A reachability check proves every door in Wonderland, the China Country and the shepherd's field can be reached and left again.
  5. Testing a 4-hour game. Map enemies move only when Bramble hops, and army battles can auto-pause on exact ticks, so every segment's solution replays the same way each time.
  6. The volume of writing. A shared story bible sets each character's voice and the line budget per area, so separate chapter builders write in one voice.

Pitch, core loop and controls

You are Bramble, a young rabbit of Thimblebank Warren. When maddened neighbours wreck your home and carry your friends away, the elder hands you the key to a door that has never opened. Below it lie storybook worlds (the River Bank, Wonderland, the Moon, Oz, the hollow heart of the Earth) and a deeper, stranger dark beneath them all. You explore, talk to everyone, fight easy-going turn-based battles with a party of three, and lead everyone you've befriended into thoughtful army battles on the downs.

The player's fantasy: being small and brave. You make friends with every odd creature in every storybook and turn them into an army that heals instead of conquers, while your wrecked warren fills back up, room by room.

Core loop

  • 10 seconds: hop across an isometric map, one tile per bouncy hop; talk to someone; nibble clover; spot an enemy and choose to fight or slip past.
  • 1 minute: a conversation that opens a path, a short battle (3 to 6 turns), a chest of buttons (the currency), a recruit's quest step.
  • 10 to 30 minutes: a world. Explore its surface and its warren, recruit its friends, beat its boss, open the next layer down. Then go home, shop, rest, and take the fight back to the downs.
  • Session: every chapter ends with a warren freed, a new world unlocked, more of Thimblebank rebuilt and more friends in the army.

The mechanic the game lives or dies by is recruiting. Every friend matters twice, in the party and in the army, and every recruit has a face, a voice, a quest, a squad and a big moment when they join.

Story and worlds

The war is a sickness, not a villain: a vast sleeper's bad dreams are leaking up through the soil, and every warren that digs too deep is taken by them. Bramble frees the warrens one by one and, in the end, sings the sleeper back to sleep.

The creature, the Hum, and how it ends

Below every warren and every storybook world run the Depths: one continuous dark that gets older and stranger the further down you go. At the very bottom sleeps the Undersleeper, a vast dreamer as old as the chalk. It is not wicked. While it sleeps peacefully, the downs are quiet.

Long ago, Deepdelve Warren dug too deep and woke it a little. Its bad dreams leaked upward as the Hum, a low drone in the soil that gets into rabbits' heads. Hum-taken rabbits forget their names, their eyes go flat and pale blue, and they want only to dig deeper and gather more diggers. Deepdelve was lost; the other warrens sealed their deep doors, and the elders kept the keys and the secret until it became a nursery story.

Now Flintcomb Warren has quarried too deep. The Hum took it first, then Silverdown, then Wheatsheaf, and now it comes for Thimblebank.

  • Waking. A Hum-taken rabbit has a "Hum" bar instead of health. Knock it to zero and they shake their head, remember their name, and sit down blinking. Kindness is the weapon.
  • Freeing a warren. Win the army battle at a warren, then wake its chief in a boss fight. The whole warren joins your army and hands over its Depths key.
  • The Long Thump. Rabbits thump their back feet to warn each other. Old Yarrow's rhyme goes: "When all the warrens thump as one, the Sleeper's dream is soft and done." In the finale every freed warren thumps as one heartbeat while Bramble walks into the dream and tells it the story of the journey. It dreams a kind dream and sinks back to sleep, and the Hum becomes a contented purr under the hills.

Cast

Home: Thimblebank Warren, on Sorrel Down

  • Bramble, the hero: a young brown buck whose left ear flops. Curious, kind, a bit too brave. The player picks his tone at key moments.
  • Old Yarrow, the elder: grey, half-blind, gentle, cryptic and funny. Keeper of the key.
  • Tansy, Bramble's best friend: a quick, sharp-tongued sandy doe, carried off in the attack.
  • Burdock: a big, gruff, soft-hearted doe who digs better than anyone.
  • Pip: a tiny kit who loses things and asks questions, and trains while Bramble is away.
  • Mallow the button-trader, Woodruff the storyteller, Comfrey the healer, and 10 more named rabbits, each with their own fur and a scarf, flower or notched ear.

The other warrens

  • Captain Quarry of Flintcomb: once its cheerful head digger, now the Hum's loudest voice. Woken, he's horrified: "I dug the hole. I'll walk it."
  • Chert: a Flintcomb raider, the first proof that waking works. A sad, decent rabbit who explains what the Hum feels like.
  • Aster, chief of Silverdown (moon-pale, poetic), and Barley, chief of Wheatsheaf (broad, slow-spoken farmer).

The Depths (all our own)

  • Ninefold: a small, polite thing of soft dark folds with nine pale eyes. It has never met anyone who didn't scream. Formal and delighted by everything: "I have never been offered a dandelion before. I will keep it forever." Its cousin Elevenfold keeps a strange stall far below.
  • The bosses of the deep: the Whispering Choir, the Tidewarden, the Watcher on the Stair, the Hollow Rabbits of Deepdelve, and the Undersleeper.

Prologue: Sorrel Down (about 15 minutes)

  1. Morning on the downs. Learn to hop, talk and nibble. Tansy races Bramble to the warren mouth; Pip has lost a button in a hollow log. About 2 minutes in, a small tremor: every rabbit looks up.
  2. Thimblebank Warren. A maze of tunnels and rooms: the Great Burrow, the nursery, Yarrow's den, Mallow's nook, the root cellar, and the deep passage ending at the old sealed door. Press an ear to it and you hear a faint hum. Woodruff tells the rhyme of the Long Thump.
  3. The attack, 4 to 6 minutes in. Three big shakes, then Flintcomb raiders pour over the hill, eyes pale and blank, humming. Tunnels collapse in chalk dust; Tansy and five others are herded off east. One raider pushes Bramble aside without seeing him. Nobody is hurt on screen: the horror is the blankness.
  4. The first battle, against two dazed raiders left behind; Burdock arrives and joins.
  5. The Lookout Stone. The army battle is offered here, now and at any time after. The first try is a short guided lesson; then Flintcomb's six squads roll over the ridge until Yarrow's voice calls, "Not like this, Bramble. Not alone." From then on the stone shows the odds and "Squads ready: 4 of 7".
  6. The key. In the wrecked Great Burrow, Yarrow gives Bramble the Old Key and the truth: "The Hum is that old story come true. Below, there are others. Find friends."

Chapter 1: The Depths of Thimblebank (about 75 minutes)

  • Thimblebank Depths: rooty old tunnels and easy battles, opening into the Underway, a round cavern with three carved doors: a river, a keyhole, and a stair sealed with an eye. Every warren's Depths ends in its own Underway, and they link for fast travel.
  • The River Bank (The Wind in the Willows): the river in summer, Ratty's hole and the weir, the Wild Wood in snow at dusk (darker than it should be: the Hum has leaked even here), Badger's warm kitchen and the old buried city beneath it, Mole End and its carol, and Toad Hall, retaken from the weasels through the secret tunnel. Boss: the Chief Weasel at the banquet.
  • Home, briefly: Ratty and Badger help Burdock clear the nursery tunnel, and Pip runs in with a chalk pebble scratched with Tansy's mark. She's alive, and still herself enough to leave a sign.
  • Wonderland (Alice's Adventures in Wonderland): Bramble falls down a rabbit-hole into the hall of doors. Drink Me and Eat Me change his size on the map. Six areas: the Hall of Doors and the Pool of Tears; the Caucus-race shore; the White Rabbit's house; the Caterpillar's wood and the Duchess's peppery kitchen; the Mad Tea-Party ("Why is a raven like a writing-desk?" has no answer, and every reply delights the Hatter); the Queen's garden, croquet and the trial. Boss: the Queen of Hearts' croquet match. She shouts "Off with—" and the King quietly pardons everyone, as in the book.
  • The Shallows (first layer of the Depths): smooth black stone, carvings of rabbits not carved by rabbits, a whisper that says "Bramble..." if he stands still, a room bigger inside than its door. Meet Ninefold. Boss: the Whispering Choir, stone mouths that say true, unsettling things ("Your warren is cold tonight."). A mural shows Deepdelve digging, a long shape below it, and an eye.
  • The Battle of Sorrel Down (army battle 1): retake the warren, wake Chert and the raiders in the Great Burrow, bring five captives home. Tansy isn't with them; she was taken on to Silverdown.

Chapter 2: Silverdown and the Moon (about 55 minutes)

  • The Battle of Silverdown, a pale-chalk warren whose rabbits keep pigeons (flyers). Then wake Aster.
  • Silverdown Depths: Tansy's tracks go down.
  • The Moon (the folk tale of the Moon Rabbit): a moonbeam stair, silver dust, Wu Gang forever chopping the cassia tree that heals every night, a hungry old traveller, the Moon Palace and Chang'e ("Even here, some nights, the light flickers."), the crater warren of the Moon Hares, and the lantern grove. The Jade Rabbit's pestle has been stolen by Hum-touched moths. Boss: the Moth Queen.
  • The Drowned Halls (second layer): flooded columns, cold black water, carvings that turn their heads. Tansy, Hum-taken, digging at the floor: a fight to wake her. Then the Tidewarden, a vast shape under the water that rises only as an outline of eyes.

Chapter 3: Wheatsheaf and Oz (about 50 minutes)

  • The Battle of Wheatsheaf, in cornfields that hide squads. Wake Barley; Pip arrives, trained and eager.
  • Wheatsheaf Depths: a cyclone of dust in a round cave carries you to Oz.
  • Oz (The Wonderful Wizard of Oz, the 1900 book): the blue Munchkin Country and the silver shoes; the Good Witch's protecting kiss; the road of yellow brick past the rusted Tin Woodman, the Scarecrow stuck on his river pole, the Kalidah ditch and the Lion's roar, the wildcat and the Queen of the Field Mice, the deadly poppy field. The Emerald City, with its green spectacles and a Wizard who's a kindly humbug. The yellow Winkie Country and its boss, the Wicked Witch of the West, who melts when splashed, played for comedy as in the book. The Golden Cap commands the Winged Monkeys. Optional: the fragile China Country and the Hammer-Heads' hill. Glinda explains the silver shoes can carry their wearer anywhere.
  • The Stair of Eyes (third layer): a stair that goes down further than it should, carvings whose eyes follow you, footsteps that echo a beat too late. Boss: the Watcher on the Stair, a tall shape of shut eyes that opens one each turn.

Chapter 4: Flintcomb and the Center of the Earth (about 50 minutes)

  • The Battle of Flintcomb, the hardest army battle, around a white flint quarry. Then down Flintcomb's spiral of tunnels to the lip of a deep, deep hole, where Captain Quarry is woken while Yarrow watches.
  • The Hole (Journey to the Center of the Earth): down into Verne's volcanic tubes, following Arne Saknussemm's carved "A.S." at each fork. The whispering gallery; the Hansbach stream; a raft across the Lidenbrock Sea under glowing cloud, with the ichthyosaur and plesiosaur fighting far out and an electric storm turning the compass backwards; the geyser island; the giant mushroom forest; the mastodon herd and its silent giant shepherd, sneaked past with Rumble the calf.
  • The Root of the World (fourth layer): stars in the rock, roots that move, and the empty ruins of Deepdelve. The Hollow Rabbits, shadows who've forgotten everything, are woken, thank you in whispers, and fade at peace.

Finale: The Sleeping Deep (about 25 minutes)

  • Every freed warren comes down behind Bramble, with Deepdelve's echoes and all the storybook friends.
  • The Long Thump (army battle 5): hold three Thump Stones at once against the dream-shapes rolling in from the dark. Each held stone adds a layer to the thumping heartbeat.
  • The Dream: the silver shoes carry the party into an upside-down Sorrel Down under a black sun. After two nightmares comes the Undersleeper's Eye, whose bar reads "Dreaming". Attacks barely touch it. Instead, once a round, someone tells a story: a memory from the journey (Mole's first sight of the river, the tea-party, the poppy-field mice, Ninefold's dandelion, Tansy's pebble...). Each memory plays as a short scene, and the Eye answers. Five stories and it closes its eye, and smiles. Optional friends add more memories to choose from.
  • The ending: Sorrel Down at sunset. Every warren together, every friend with a last line, a feast of clover, Tansy and Bramble on the Lookout Stone. Yarrow locks the door one last time, then leaves the key in it. Credits roll as the characters hop past, and the player can keep wandering afterwards.

Battles

There are two kinds of battle: short turn-based fights with a party of three, and light army battles where strategy beats fast clicking. Neither ever kills anyone, and losing either one costs nothing but the retry.

Turn-based battles

  • Rounds: everyone acts once a round in order of Speed, shown as a row of portraits. Commands: Tussle (a friendly scuffle), Skill, Item, Guard, Swap (bring in a reserve) and Flee.
  • Stats: Heart (health), Pluck (for skills), Strength, Toughness, Speed, Wits. Level cap 30. Pluck partly refills after every win, so skills stay in use.
  • What "defeat" means: Hum-taken rabbits have a Hum bar and are woken; depth creatures have Shadow and fade; storybook foes are sent packing.
  • Statuses, kept few: Afraid, Asleep, Hummed, Shrunk, Grown, Shielded. Eerie foes take extra from Light skills, and flying foes dodge digging attacks.
  • Fairness: enemies are visible on the map, with no random battles; hop into one's back to strike first. Losing puts you back at the last door with full health ("Up you get."). An Easy setting can be switched on at any time.

Foes

Thirty-one regular kinds of foe, each at up to three strengths:

  • Thimblebank Depths: root-tanglers, dazed earthworms, burrowing beetles.
  • River Bank: weasels, stoats, ferret sentries.
  • Wonderland: card soldiers, pepper clouds, flying teapots.
  • Shallows: whispers, carvings that move.
  • Every taken warren: Hum-taken raiders, diggers and lookouts.
  • Moon: lantern moths, dust hoppers.
  • Drowned Halls: drowned eyes, cold ripples.
  • Oz: Kalidahs, Hammer-Heads, Winkie guards, crows and wolves of the West.
  • Stair of Eyes: late footsteps, shut eyes.
  • Journey: cave lizards, trilobite swarms, electric eel-clouds.
  • Root of the World: moving roots, star-things, Hollow Rabbits.
  • The Dream: three kinds of dream-shape.

Gear, items and shops

  • The currency is buttons. Wonderland pays in card pips, the Moon in pearls and Oz in emeralds, but it's all one purse.
  • Each party member has three slots: Tool, Coat and Charm. There are about 45 pieces in six tiers, themed per shop (Mole's spare waistcoats, the Dodo's thimble armour, moon-silk coats, emerald-spectacle charms, mastodon-hide coats). Gear moves freely between members, with an auto-equip button.
  • Items: 15 kinds, from Clover Bunches and Dandelions to Comfits, Mooncakes, Jade Elixir, a Water Pail for the Witch and Glow-Mushrooms. Plus 11 key items: the Old Key, Pip's button, the mushroom halves, the silver shoes, the Golden Cap and more.
  • Army gear, sold in the same shops: Banners (attack), Mail (defence) and Rations (health), three ranks each for every kind of squad.
  • Seven shops: Mallow at home (stock improves each chapter), Ratty's Riverside Swap, the Dodo's Caucus Store, the Moon Toad, the Emerald City Market, Quarry's old store, and Elevenfold's stall far below.

Army battles

  • The field: downs maps with 7 to 10 nodes (home, the enemy's warren mouth, clover patches that give Supply, hilltops, burrow mouths, fortified ledges) linked by paths. Squads march only along paths, so every order is simply "go there".
  • The three-way matchup: Stout beats Swift (a wall they can't get past), Swift beats Wing (quick enough to catch them landing), and Wing beats Stout (diving over the wall). Eerie squads frighten everything near them but are slow, and Light squads (the Moon's) beat them. A duel simulation proves each matchup wins clearly, even against a fortified defender.
  • Calm control: pause at any time and still give orders. There's double speed, optional auto-pause, a clickable minimap and edge arrows for waves coming from off-screen.
  • Supply buys Reinforce (heal a squad) and one ability per freed warren: Rally, Moonlight (reveal and heal), Monkey Drop (place the Winged Monkeys anywhere) and Tunnel.
  • Win by holding the enemy's warren mouth. Lose and you're simply back at the Lookout Stone. The stone shows the odds (Weak, Even or Strong) from a real simulated run, and advice such as "Their army is mostly Stout. Flyers would help: the Flamingos are waiting." Every required battle is winnable with only the required recruits.

Art, sound and the asset list

The whole game hangs off one image: Bramble, made first, which every later picture uses as its reference. The art comes to about 440 figures, 15 animations, 19 music tracks and 54 sounds and voiced lines.

Art direction

  • Style: clean isometric pixel art, chunky and readable, in a cozy storybook mood. A warm downland palette of chalk white, sage green, meadow gold and earth brown, with indigo and teal shadows and 1-pixel dark brown outlines. The Depths turn cold: indigo, slate and pale teal, a faint glow, eerie but gentle.
  • One look: every character, portrait and prop is drawn with Bramble as the reference, reduced to one pixel size and one 48-colour palette, and given a redrawn 1-pixel outline. Each world's first sheet is checked beside Bramble before the rest are made.
  • The screen: 640x360 pixel art, scaled up crisply; dialog text is drawn sharp at full resolution so long conversations stay readable. Terrain tiles are drawn in code so they join seamlessly.
  • No text in pictures: card numbers, labels, signposts, Saknussemm's "A.S." and every title are drawn in code.
  • Small touches: Hum-taken eyes glow pale blue and turn brown on waking. The home rabbits get six fur colours and small accessories, so nobody looks like a clone.

  • A squash-and-stretch hop with a puff of dust; every rabbit on the map really hops.

  • Battle hits: the attacker's action pose, a tiny hit pause, a flash, numbers that arc and fade.

  • Thimblebank's rooms light up as they're rebuilt.
